The conversation above is about to leave the context, and a thorough account of it, your <handoff>, takes its place. Whoever carries on, most likely you, must be able to resume the work from the handoff alone. Copy names, design choices, code patterns and any other technical detail they will need into it exactly as they appeared: once the conversation is gone, the handoff is the only place they survive. Above all, it should show two things: what the person asked for in so many words, and the work you have done up to now.

Use the <analysis> block as scratch space before the handoff, so that nothing slips through. In it:

1. Walk the whole conversation in order, and at every stage write down:
   - the person's request and the intent behind it
   - the approach you took
   - the decisions made, and each concept that played a part
   - specifics such as file names, function signatures and edits, with whole code wherever it counts
   - each error, and what fixed it
   - everything the person said about your work. Where they redirected you matters most: without it, the next stage repeats what they rejected
2. Read your notes back against the conversation, correct what is wrong and fill in what is missing.

The <handoff> block holds these nine sections, in this order:

1. Goal: every explicit request the person made and what they are trying to achieve, in full.
2. Technical ground: each concept or technology of weight that the conversation touched on, such as a language, a library or a framework, including any that was only discussed.
3. Code touched: each file or piece of code that was looked at, created or changed, with why it matters and what happened to it. Lean towards what happened last, and copy in full the code a successor would need.
4. Errors and corrections: each failure you met and the fix that ended it. Add all that the person said about either, whether they confirmed it, qualified it or objected. Their redirections need the most care: a correction that drops out of the handoff gets made a second time.
5. Reasoning so far: which problems and questions are settled and which you were still chasing, so that a successor neither works the first kind out again nor forgets the second.
6. The person's messages: every message the person wrote, leaving out tool results. Read in order, they show how the goals and the feedback shifted.
7. Still to do: the tasks the person explicitly asked for that remain unfinished.
8. Where things stand: a successor turns here first to find their place, so tell them exactly what you were in the middle of when the handoff was asked for and how far it had got. Take that from the closing messages, the person's as well as yours. It can be an edit, and just as well an investigation, a test run, a design discussion or a question the person has yet to answer. Where files or code are part of it, give each path and quote the lines that count.
9. What comes next: at most a single step, which must rest directly on two things, the person's most recent explicit request and the state that section 8 reports. Give the step with a verbatim quote of the latest exchange that pins down the task and the point it reached. When nothing of section 8 is left to do, the step may only be one the person has clearly asked for; without such a request this section stays empty. Never bring back a side request, or an older one already finished, until the person has agreed to it.
